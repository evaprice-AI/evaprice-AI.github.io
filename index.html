About

I'm a final-year PhD candidate in Biochemical Engineering at University College London, working where biological science meets machine learning. My thesis — Next Generation Viral Vector Design via Liquid Machine Learning Reinforced with Pan-omics — integrates machine learning with multi-omics data to find the molecular features that govern cell line stability and performance, and to turn those into engineering targets for the cells that manufacture gene therapies.

Much of the work has been infrastructure as much as analysis: assembling a multi-omics resource where none existed, architecting the cloud environment to process it, and building workflows other people can actually re-run. That has made me something of a convert to data stewardship, FAIR principles and open science — I now hold a data stewardship post alongside the PhD, and I care a great deal about research being reproducible by someone other than its author.

I came to this from genetics in Dublin by way of precision-medicine data analytics, and I've since worked across regulation, clinical AI and startup building. I'm Irish, based in London, and the basking shark is here because it is the largest fish in Irish waters and feeds by filtering enormous volumes of water for very small signal — which is, more or less, the job description.

02

PhD research

Cell and gene therapies can treat disease at its genetic origin, but making them is expensive and hard to scale. The bottleneck is viral-vector production, which depends on living producer cells that behave far less predictably than conventional manufacturing systems. My work takes HEK293 — the workhorse cell line for vector production — and asks what its molecular biology would have to look like for us to engineer it deliberately.

01 — Resource

A multi-omics database that didn't exist

No dataset of sufficient scale existed, so the first stage was building one: genomic, epigenomic, transcriptomic, proteomic and metabolomic data drawn from eight public databases into a resource holding over 100 TB of compressed raw data — each layer needing its own processing route, and inconsistent metadata fighting back the whole way.

02 — Semantics

Why the machines can't read our papers

Early LLMs proved unsuitable for automated data cleaning, largely because researchers describe their data in formats that aren't machine readable. I quantified that problem and proposed an intelligent search-string construction method, validated on multi-omics literature retrieval.

03 — Infrastructure

Becoming the cloud engineer

UCL's HPC architecture couldn't accommodate the scale, so I built the compute environment in AWS from scratch — architecture, storage, security, scaling and cost — with analyses implemented in Nextflow for reproducibility. The result was adopted as a UCL CDI AWS case study for cloud-enabled multi-omics research.

04 — The layers

Every omics layer, one cell line

I work across the full biological cascade — genomics, epigenomics, transcriptomics, proteomics and metabolomics — analysing each layer of HEK293 in turn, and each one has surfaced potential avenues for cell-line engineering. But the layers interact, so taken separately they only give part of the picture. Multi-omic integration is the next stage, coming soon.

03

Synapsid

A machine-learning approach to laboratory reproducibility — cutting the research effort that gets silently duplicated across labs because the first attempt was never findable, never legible, or never published.

It comes directly out of the PhD: three years of trying to reuse other people's omics data is a thorough education in how much work gets done twice. Currently in development.
